Linkage Design

Build a mechanism.

Place joints, tie them into rigid bodies, ground some, drive one with a motor, and run it. The 3D view follows along, and the Blender export gives you the animated skeleton to build printable geometry around. Everything runs in your browser — nothing is uploaded.

Ask the assistant, or tell it what to do

Commands are executed exactly and need no model at all: try “load the Hoeken and run it”, “make the motor faster”, “turn gravity on” or “what am I looking at”. Prose questions need a small language model, which downloads into this browser once and then answers on your own GPU, with nothing sent anywhere.

How to use it

Building

Every one of these is also a button in the toolbar, and each greys out until it has something to act on — L needs two joints selected, S exactly three, M a selected link with one grounded joint. Editing is disabled entirely while the mechanism is running: press R to stop first.

Click
place a joint on empty space, or select one
Shift-click
add to the selection instead of replacing it — on an existing joint, or on empty space to place one and keep the rest selected
Drag
from empty space to box-select; from a joint to move the selection
Click a bar
select that link
L
join the selected joints into one rigid body — two makes a bar, three or more makes a plate
S
hold a joint on a rail: select three, and the first becomes a pin held on the line through the other two. Ground the two rail joints with A and it is a slideway; leave them on a moving link and it is a pin running in that link's slot
A
ground the selection, so it never moves
M
drive the selected link with a motor. It needs exactly one grounded joint, which becomes the pivot
+ / −
change the motor's speed
V
let a link change length. It still holds its length until the geometry leaves it no choice
T

Delete

When it binds

Fixed-length links here are genuinely rigid. If the motor reaches a position the mechanism cannot physically assume — a non-Grashof linkage hitting its limit, say — the run stops there rather than stretching a bar, and tells you. To get through it, change the geometry or make a link variable with V.

Exporting

Two exports, and they are different things. The Blender script writes an animation: one cylinder per link edge and one empty per joint, keyframed through a full cycle. Open it in Blender's Scripting tab and press Space. It is the mechanism's skeleton, for you to build real geometry around.

The STL parts write objects: a zip holding one solid per part — link plates with a boss at every pin, slider rails with a slot cut through them, headed pins, push-on caps, spacer washers, and a baseplate that holds the anchors — plus a MANIFEST.txt saying what each one is and how the pile stacks up. Parts that share a pin are put on separate layers and any gap left on a pin is packed out with a spacer, so nothing rubs and nothing floats. Every solid is checked watertight before it is written; one that fails is left out and said so in the manifest rather than handed to a slicer that would quietly print something else. One world unit is one millimetre throughout.

The zip also carries assembly.stl and assembly_exploded.stl. Neither is a part to print — they are several solids touching, not one shell — but opening them is the quickest way to see which piece goes where.

The solver is a Levenberg–Marquardt damped Gauss–Newton iteration over pairwise rest-distance constraints, warm-started from the previous frame — which is what keeps the motion on one branch without a special case for four-bar ambiguity, and what lets it handle open chains, multi-loop mechanisms and plates rather than just four-bars. It is a JavaScript port of Linkage-Design, my desktop tool in C, and reproduces its results to the last decimal place.
